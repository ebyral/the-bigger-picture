The Bigger Picture

Exploring health, mortality and the questions that could improve prevention

Our health is shaped by many things. Our medical conditions, the treatments we receive, our experiences with healthcare, our circumstances, and the places where we live all form part of the picture.

Yet when we examine someone's health, we often focus on a few pieces at a time. A blood-pressure reading. A blood sugar result. A diagnosis.

What might we discover if we brought more of these pieces together?

Could seeing more of the picture help us recognize risks earlier—and eventually prevent avoidable deaths?

Using longitudinal health records from OCHIN, a US safety-net health network, we linked clinical, healthcare, behavioral, social, neighborhood and mortality information for more than 465,000 adults living with diabetes and hypertension. We then used predictive modeling to explore how these characteristics relate to mortality.

We found relationships worth looking at more closely: clinical measurements sometimes followed unexpected patterns, and neighborhood circumstances were also associated with mortality. They offer clues about what may be missed in a single snapshot—and what we could investigate to improve prevention. Now it’s your turn to explore.

05 / What the bigger picture helped us see

A wider view raises better questions.

A measurement at one visit can tell us a lot. Looking across health histories, healthcare experiences and the circumstances in which people live helps us examine a wider range of clues about why outcomes differ.

In this study, blood pressure and BMI showed more complex patterns than simple straight lines, and neighborhood circumstances were associated with mortality. Looking across these layers gave us more to investigate.

Seeing more of the picture can help us ask better questions about who might be at risk, what we may be missing, and where prevention could make a difference.

What would help us go further?

We would need richer information about people’s health and care, and further studies to test which changes actually prevent harm. These findings help guide those next questions.

About the research

How was this studied?

Study details, comparisons and limitations for anyone who wants to dig deeper.

The study and outcome

Longitudinal records for 465,799 adults living with diabetes and hypertension in a US safety-net network, linked with neighborhood and mortality information. The outcome was recorded death, modeled using a person-time-offset Poisson generalized additive model (GAM). Time-varying predictors were lagged by one quarter.

Predictor selection and scope

We began with 69 possible characteristics and used cross-validation and a regularized selection procedure to choose 40 for the final model. A characteristic’s inclusion means it was considered useful to keep in the joint analysis. It does not show that changing it would improve someone’s health. One term could not be estimated separately because it repeated information already in the model.

Held-out evaluation

In 93,154 held-out people, 4,423 deaths were observed and 4,389.8 predicted, yielding an observed-to-expected ratio of approximately 1.008. This indicates close agreement in the aggregate; it does not by itself establish individual-level discrimination or calibration across groups.

Associations, explanations and decision-making

Displayed rate ratios are adjusted associations. The study is observational, and healthcare encounters, medications and the timing of measurements may reflect underlying health and clinical decisions. Moving a slider is an exploration of a reported relationship and does not estimate the outcome of an intervention.

Why include so many characteristics?

The featured findings help visitors get started. The full list shows everything included in the analysis. The strength and certainty of an association, and whether a characteristic helps prediction, are separate questions.

Current data limitations

The figures display adjusted comparisons from the completed study. New numerical estimates at every possible slider position need the final model’s exported curve data, so the current controls help you inspect the reported figures. The final numerical fit and uncertainty should be checked before public publication.
